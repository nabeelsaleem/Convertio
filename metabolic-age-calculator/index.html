How the Metabolic Age Calculator Works

Your chronological age counts the years since your birth, whereas metabolic age is a fitness-oriented way to compare your estimated resting metabolic rate against typical reference values for different adult age groups. Because basal energy expenditure gradually declines across adulthood as lean muscle mass tends to decrease, matching your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) to an age-cohort reference curve offers a simple snapshot of how your estimated resting burn compares to peers of the same sex.

To calculate metabolic age transparently without hidden black-box adjustments, this tool first computes your Mifflin-St Jeor BMR from your sex, age, height, and weight, then maps that number onto a sex-specific age-cohort reference BMR curve. It also doubles as a practical calorie intake calculator by multiplying your resting BMR by your chosen daily activity factor to estimate maintenance calories (Total Daily Energy Expenditure, or TDEE).

Mifflin-St Jeor Formula & Age-Cohort Reference Method

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation (1990) is widely regarded as one of the most reliable predictive equations for estimating adult resting energy expenditure from basic body measurements. All imperial inputs (feet, inches, and pounds) are automatically converted to centimeters and kilograms before applying the equations below:

Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) + 5

Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) − 161

Age-Cohort Reference BMR: Male = 1,740 − 7 × (age − 20) kcal/day  |  Female = 1,390 − 6 × (age − 20) kcal/day

Estimated Metabolic Age: Solved by finding the cohort age whose reference BMR equals your Mifflin-St Jeor BMR, bounded to 15–90 years.

The reference curve reflects both the direct Mifflin-St Jeor age slope (−5 kcal/day per year) and typical age-related changes in adult lean mass across cohorts from age 20 to 80:

Worked Example

Consider a 35-year-old male who is 175 cm tall (5 ft 9 in), weighs 75 kg (165.3 lb), and exercises moderately 3 to 5 days per week (activity multiplier 1.55):

  1. Calculate Mifflin-St Jeor BMR: (10 × 75) + (6.25 × 175) − (5 × 35) + 5 = 750 + 1,093.75 − 175 + 5 = 1,673.75 kcal/day (rounded to 1,674 kcal/day).
  2. Find Age-35 Male Cohort Reference BMR: 1,740 − 7 × (35 − 20) = 1,635 kcal/day.
  3. Compare Against Cohort Curve: 20 + (1,740 − 1,673.75) ÷ 7 = 29.46, which rounds to an estimated metabolic age of 29 years (6 years younger than chronological age).
  4. Estimate Daily Calorie Intake: 1,673.75 × 1.55 = 2,594 kcal/day for weight maintenance.

Formula & Limitations

Because metabolic age is not a standardized medical metric, every method comes with clear trade-offs that are worth keeping in mind:

  • Total weight vs. body composition: The Mifflin-St Jeor equation uses total body weight and cannot distinguish between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. A taller or heavier person automatically gets a higher absolute BMR, which can push a pure BMR-to-cohort estimate younger even if body fat is elevated, while a very petite adult can receive an older estimate despite healthy fitness levels.
  • Comparison to smart scales and wearables: Consumer bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) scales and wearables—such as Tanita, InBody, or Hume Health devices—send a mild electrical current through the body to estimate fat-free mass and then apply proprietary algorithms and internal databases. Those estimates can fluctuate with hydration, meal timing, and skin moisture, and they rarely match formula-only calculators.
  • Comparison to clinical testing: In clinical or sports-science settings, resting energy expenditure is measured directly via indirect calorimetry (oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production), and body composition is assessed with DEXA scanning. Even with lab measurements, converting resting burn into a "metabolic age" still depends on which reference population is selected.
  • Unmeasured physiological factors: Predictive equations do not account for thyroid function, genetics, medications, sleep debt, hormonal shifts, or metabolic adaptation from prolonged calorie restriction. Treat the output as a starting benchmark for tracking habits over time rather than medical advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

There is no single clinical standard for metabolic age, so most online calculators estimate it in two steps. First, they compute your resting metabolic rate (BMR) using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation from your sex, age, height, and weight. Second, they compare your estimated BMR against an age-cohort reference BMR table to find the age group whose typical resting calorie burn most closely matches yours.

Hume Health devices, such as the Hume Body Pod smart scale and Hume Band, use bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and proprietary wellness algorithms rather than a height-and-weight equation alone. They combine estimated body composition metrics such as lean mass and body fat with physiological signals and internal age-group reference datasets, which is why a Hume score often differs from a Mifflin-St Jeor calculator estimate.

In general wellness tracking, a metabolic age estimate equal to or lower than your chronological age suggests your estimated resting calorie burn meets or exceeds the reference average for your age cohort. A higher estimate simply means your calculated BMR sits below that cohort benchmark and is best interpreted alongside body composition, activity level, and overall health markers.

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is the estimated number of calories your body uses at complete rest to maintain vital functions like breathing and circulation. Daily calorie intake, or Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), multiplies your BMR by an activity factor to estimate how many calories you need each day to maintain your current weight given your movement and exercise habits.

Different calculators and smart scales rely on different BMR equations (such as Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict, or Katch-McArdle), different body composition assumptions, and separate reference databases. Because no universal reference table exists, comparing trends over time on the same tool is more meaningful than comparing single readings across different brands.
